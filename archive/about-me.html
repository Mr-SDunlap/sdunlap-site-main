Snapshot

The_Overlap

My interest in architecture was never really about buildings — it was about how people move through and occupy space, and how much you can do by working smart within real constraints: local materials, non-destructive methods, a plan that holds up under pressure. Those are the same questions I ask now, just pointed at screens instead of structures.

01

Floor Plans → Wireframes

A floor plan is a constraint diagram before it's anything else — circulation, sightlines, load-bearing walls. A wireframe does the same job for a screen: what has to be reachable in one move, what's structural, what can flex.

Richard Saul Wurman, trained as an architect, coined the term "Information Architecture" in 1976 — the field wireframing grew out of.

02

Pattern Language → Design Systems

Reusable, named solutions to recurring problems — a stair detail that works across a hundred houses, a card component that works across a hundred screens.

Christopher Alexander's "A Pattern Language" (1977) directly inspired "Design Patterns: Elements of Reusable Object-Oriented Software" (1994) — the book that gave software design systems their vocabulary.

03

Wayfinding → UX Flows

Legible paths, edges, and landmarks that let a stranger navigate without a map.

Kevin Lynch's wayfinding research in "The Image of the City" (1960) — the same problem site navigation and user flows solve in a browser.

04

Material Constraints → Tech Stack

Deciding what a building is made of, and why, before drawing continues. Picking a stack is the same discipline poured into different material.

The Bauhaus (founded 1919 under Walter Gropius) built its entire curriculum on merging architectural and material discipline with design — the same integration this is describing.
